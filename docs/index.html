Publication details

Purpose

This report helps financial institutions, market infrastructure operators, digital-asset businesses, regulators and central banks understand what the quantum transition means for them now. It deals with two practical questions. The first is the security change that has already started. The second is the chance to test quantum and quantum-inspired methods on selected financial problems, and to build the capacity an institution will need to capture quantum advantage.

No technical background is assumed. The report separates what is in use today, what is being tested and what remains research, and the reference list at the end gives the source of every result it cites.

Who this report is for

The report is written for the whole quantum finance ecosystem. Its two main audiences are financial institutions and the quantum industry, but regulators, researchers and others will also find sections written for them.

Executive summary

Most financial leaders first meet quantum computing as a conference theme or a headline about the next frontier after artificial intelligence. That impression hides work already under way. Leading banks, insurers and asset managers have research teams, security programmes and partnerships with quantum companies, and some have tested quantum methods on real trading and portfolio problems. Many others are still unsure where quantum fits, what it will cost, or when it will matter.

Money and attention are following. McKinsey estimates that quantum computing could create between $1.3 trillion and $2.7 trillion of economic value worldwide by 2035, with $400 billion to $600 billion of it in financial services [42, 43]. Investment in quantum start-ups reached $12.6 billion in 2025, more than six times the year before [42]. Preparation lags well behind: in a 2025 ISACA poll of more than 2,600 digital trust professionals, only 5 per cent said their organisation had a defined quantum computing strategy [44].

This report addresses two separate questions. The first is resilience. A large enough quantum computer would break the cryptography that protects payments, customer data, digital identities and signed instructions. That machine does not exist yet, but data copied today can be opened later, and replacing the cryptography across a financial institution and its suppliers takes five years or more. Governments and supervisors now point to about 2030 for the most sensitive systems and 2035 for the rest [7–10]. The work therefore needs to start now, and it can: the replacement standards were published in 2024 and run on ordinary computers [1].

The second question is advantage. Quantum computers are expected to solve some financial problems better or faster than today’s computers: building portfolios under many constraints, spotting fraud, pricing complex products and measuring risk. The evidence is promising but uneven. A few trials on real data have equalled or beaten their classical comparisons [24, 26]; other studies show classical methods still ahead, and the clearest theoretical gains need machines far larger than any built so far [30]. The sensible course is to test carefully against strong classical benchmarks, and not to wait.

For the next 12 to 24 months, institutions should name an accountable owner for quantum readiness, map where vulnerable cryptography is used, ask critical suppliers for dated commitments, and pick one or two business problems worth testing. The short self-assessment in Section 1 is a starting point.

The Global Finance & Technology Network (GFTN) and its partners have built Q-FINEX, a quantum finance testbed developed with the University of Western Australia and ST Engineering, to help institutions take these steps. The Q-FINEX Quantum Finance Challenge, launched at the Singapore FinTech Festival in November 2026, invites institutions to submit real problems and teams worldwide to work on them across four proposed tracks: optimisation, quantum machine learning, simulation and digital assets. Selected results will appear as case studies in the second edition of this report, to be released at Black Swan Summit in Perth, 5 to 7 April 2027.

For financial institutions that have not yet started, the message is that the first steps are affordable and well understood, and that they do not have to be taken alone. Q-FINEX offers a self-assessment, expert briefings and a trusted network of researchers and peers to learn from. For quantum computing and post-quantum security companies, finance is one of the largest markets at stake, and its institutions are now looking for partners who can show results on real problems. Q-FINEX, the Challenge and Black Swan Summit put the two sides in the same room.

1. The transition has begun, but adoption is uneven

Quantum physics already sits inside modern finance. Transistors run its computers, lasers carry its data through fibre-optic cables, and atomic clocks keep its trading systems in time. These belong to what is often called the first quantum revolution. The second involves controlling individual quantum states, and it is producing new kinds of computers, communication links and sensors.

For financial institutions, two consequences matter most. A capable quantum computer could break the public-key cryptography that secures confidential information, payments and identities. And quantum computers offer new ways to tackle certain calculations, such as portfolio construction, derivative pricing and fraud detection. The first calls for preparation now. The second calls for careful testing against the best classical alternatives. Quantum sensing has narrower uses in finance and is outside the scope of this report.

Quantum computers in plain language

An ordinary computer stores information as bits, each either 0 or 1. A quantum computer uses qubits, which can hold a blend of 0 and 1 at the same time and can be linked so that they behave as one system.

A common description says a quantum computer tries every answer at once and picks the best. That is not quite right. A quantum computer runs a carefully designed sequence in which wrong answers tend to cancel out and right answers reinforce each other, rather like ripples on a pond. This only works for problems with the right mathematical structure. For those problems, the gain can be very large; for most everyday computing, there is none.

Factoring the large numbers behind today’s encryption happens to be one of those problems, which is why security comes first. Some finance tasks, such as estimating averages in risk simulations, also have the right structure. Others, such as many optimisation problems, may benefit, but this has to be shown case by case.

Quantum computers already exist and can be rented through the cloud. Today’s machines are small and make frequent errors, so most finance work at this stage is experimental, run alongside classical computers rather than in place of them.

From research to experiments

Some institutions have moved from reading about quantum to testing it. In September 2025, HSBC and IBM reported that a hybrid quantum and classical approach improved the prediction of whether a corporate bond quote would be filled by up to 34 per cent, compared with the classical methods used in their trial [24]. The result does not show that quantum computers will lift trading profits at scale, but it is a concrete example of quantum methods being tested on a recognisable financial problem with real data. The published paper is more cautious than the headline: the gain appeared only on real, noisy quantum hardware, a noise-free simulation did slightly worse than the classical method, and the authors say the role of noise is not yet fully understood [46]. That is why independent testing matters.

The numbers behind the attention

Interest in quantum finance rests on large estimates of future value. McKinsey’s 2026 Quantum Technology Monitor puts the economic value quantum computing could create by 2035 at $1.3 trillion to $2.7 trillion across all industries [42], and McKinsey research puts the share for financial services at $400 billion to $600 billion [43]. These are scenario estimates built from expert judgement, not forecasts of revenue, and they assume the technology matures as hoped. They are best read as a measure of what is at stake for institutions that are ready, rather than as a promise.

Spending is real today. Quantum computing companies earned more than $1 billion in revenue in 2025, and one-third of the large companies McKinsey studied spent more than $10 million on quantum computing that year [42]. Confidence has now reached deal-making scale: in late October 2026, one of the world’s largest semiconductor companies agreed to buy a quantum software company for a reported $2.3 billion, the largest acquisition the sector has yet seen, as part of a plan to offer quantum hardware and software together.

For institutions starting now, the priority is to set clear ownership, understand security exposure and choose worthwhile experiments. The Bank for International Settlements (BIS) recommends building awareness, governance and a full inventory of cryptography as the foundation for the transition [15].

Two quantum finance agendas

The rest of the report follows these two agendas: Section 2 covers resilience, Section 3 the four areas where quantum advantage is being tested, Section 4 the rules that apply, and Part III how Q-FINEX and the Quantum Finance Challenge can help.

Quick start: assess your quantum readiness

Five questions help an institution see where to begin. They cover security exposure, business bottlenecks, ownership, capability and the cost of delay. Mark each one as a clear answer, a partial answer or a question that needs investigation.

If most answers are clear, use this report to check your approach against the examples and timelines here. If several are uncertain, use it as a starting guide, and begin with the questions you cannot yet answer.

You do not need a quantum team to begin. The first steps are organisational rather than technical: name someone senior to own the topic, ask your technology team where vulnerable cryptography is used, ask your three or four most important suppliers about their plans, and arrange one briefing for the board. None of these needs significant new budget, and together they show how large the task is.

The same questions are available in the Q-FINEX self-assessment app, and updates are posted on the Q-FINEX website. The app takes a few minutes, gives a simple readiness profile and suggests a next step: an executive briefing, a readiness review, or help defining a security or business problem to test. It is also the easiest way into the wider Q-FINEX network described in Part III.

2. Quantum resilience: securing the financial system

“Transitioning to quantum-resistant algorithms today is the safeguard we need to protect against future threats.”

Morgan Stern, Effort Lead for Quantum Resistance, US National Security Agency, 1 October 2026 [41]

Think of public-key cryptography as the locks and wax seals of digital finance. The lock lets two parties who have never met agree on a secret key across the open internet. The seal proves who sent a message and that nobody has altered it. Both are at work every time a customer taps a card, a treasurer releases a payment or an adviser logs in to a client portal. A large enough quantum computer could pick the lock and forge the seal.

That machine does not yet exist, and nobody can say with confidence when it will. Two things will not wait for it. Adversaries can copy encrypted data today and open it later, and replacing the locks and seals across a bank, insurer or pension fund, and across the suppliers it depends on, takes years rather than months.

In brief

What is at risk: the methods known as RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography, which protect almost every digital transaction and identity. Everyday data encryption (AES) remains sound.

Why act now: data stolen today can be read later, and a full changeover takes five years or more. Authorities point to about 2030 for the most sensitive systems and 2035 for everything else [7–10].

What replaces it: new international standards, published in August 2024, that run on existing computers [1]. Most firms will use old and new methods side by side during the change.

Who is accountable: the institution, including for technology run by its suppliers.

What the first year should deliver: a named owner, a map of where vulnerable methods are used, a view of which data must stay private longest, dated commitments from key suppliers, a tested pilot and a funded plan.

2.1 What is at risk, in plain terms

Quantum computing threatens finance in two distinct ways, and each calls for a different response [15, 16].

Harvest now, read later. Imagine a business rival photocopying a sealed envelope today and filing it away until they can afford the key. Security specialists call this harvest now, decrypt later (HNDL). A loan file sent to a broker, a client’s medical history passed to a reinsurer, or board papers on an acquisition could be recorded in transit now and opened years from now. Nothing appears to go wrong at the time; the damage surfaces later, when the information may still be sensitive.

Forgery. The second risk is impersonation. Digital signatures are how a bank knows a payment instruction really came from its client, how an ATM knows a software update came from its manufacturer, and how a custodian knows a transfer was authorised. Once those can be forged, an attacker does not need to steal anything; they can simply pose as someone trusted. This risk arrives only with a capable quantum computer, but replacing every seal across a market takes years.

Two points keep this in proportion. The encryption that protects files and databases is not itself the weak spot; the weakness lies in how the keys to that encryption were exchanged or stored, which often relies on the vulnerable methods. And urgency depends on how long information must stay private. A share order matters for minutes, a mortgage file for 25 years, a life policy or pension record for a lifetime.

2.2 Timing: what matters is the shelf-life of your data

Executives often ask when quantum computers will arrive. A more useful question is how long our information must stay private, and how long it will take us to change. Cryptographers put it as a simple test, known as Mosca’s inequality: if the years information must stay confidential, plus the years needed to migrate, is greater than the years until a code-breaking quantum computer exists, that information is already exposed.

Consider a bank that sends a 25-year mortgage file to its loan servicer this week over an ordinary encrypted link, and expects its own migration to take five years. That file must stay private well into the 2050s. If a capable quantum computer appears at any point before then, a copy taken today can be opened. Insurers holding life and health records, and pension funds holding members' details for 40 years or more, face the same arithmetic.

When?

Nobody can exactly date the arrival of such a machine, but the estimated effort keeps falling. In 2019, Craig Gidney and Martin Ekerå estimated that breaking a standard RSA-2048 key would need around 20 million qubits; in 2025 Gidney put the figure below one million [4]; and in February 2026 a team at Iceberg Quantum claimed a new hardware design could bring it under 100,000, although no such machine exists [51]. Google and Cloudflare, which follow this research closely, have both committed to finishing their own migrations by 2029 [5, 53]. The likely pattern is a slow and costly start aimed at the most valuable targets, widening as costs fall, rather than a single day on which everything fails [17].

Expert opinion has shifted too. In the Global Risk Institute’s 2025 survey, 26 leading experts put the chance of a code-breaking quantum computer within 10 years at 28 to 49 per cent, rising to 51 to 70 per cent within 15 years [45]. Hardware makers now publish dated plans: IBM aims to deliver a machine with 200 error-corrected qubits in 2029 [49], while breaking the elliptic-curve keys behind many digital signatures is estimated to need about 1,200 to 1,450 logical qubits [22]. The gap is still large, but it is closing on a published schedule.

Their dates differ, and most are guidance rather than law for private firms, but the message is consistent: protect the most sensitive systems by about 2030 and finish by 2035. A group operating in several markets should plan to the earliest date that applies to it. Section 4 sets out what each authority expects.

2.3 The replacement: new locks already exist

The replacements exist and run on the computers institutions already own; nobody needs a quantum computer of their own. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) published the main new standards in August 2024, after an eight-year international competition [1]. Their technical names matter less than the jobs they do.

Most institutions will run old and new methods together during the changeover, so that a connection stays safe as long as either one holds. Much of this is already happening without big announcements. Modern web browsers use this hybrid protection by default, and by February 2026 more than 60 per cent of web clients could use it [6]. The catch is that this usually covers only the customer’s journey to the institution’s front door. The connections behind it, between a bank and its card processor, a fund and its custodian, or an insurer and its reinsurers, are mostly still unprotected, and they carry the most valuable data.

It’s a process, not a one-off fix: build your agility

Two promising candidates in NIST’s competition were broken late in the process using ordinary computers [17]. Standards will be refined and, occasionally, replaced. The lasting goal is what specialists call crypto-agility: being able to change these locks the way a facilities manager re-keys a building, as a routine task, rather than knocking down walls each time.

2.4 Suppliers: ask for evidence, not reassurance

Most of a financial institution’s cryptography lives inside things it buys: the core banking or policy administration system, the cloud platform, the payment network, the specialised hardware that stores keys. Outsourcing the technology does not outsource the responsibility [12, 15]. A supplier describing its product as quantum-safe is like a builder describing a house as fire-safe: reassuring, but not something a board would sign off without the certificate. Ask critical suppliers for five things:

  1. A list of the cryptography each product version uses, so you are not guessing.
  2. Named versions and dates for support of the new standards, and when versions without it stop being supported.
  3. An explanation of how your stored data is protected, including whether the keys guarding customer records rely on the vulnerable methods, and the plan to change them.
  4. Test results showing the new methods run on your network without slowing transactions or dropping connections, and the right to test together.
  5. Contract terms with delivery dates aligned to the strictest regulator you answer to, notice of changes, and remedies if dates slip.

Be wary of claims that name no specific standard, of home-grown proprietary algorithms, and of advisers who want broad access to live customer data simply to check readiness. Good discovery tools only need to see how systems are configured, not the data they hold [17].

2.5 The first 12 months

The first year is about learning enough to make sound decisions and to put a credible budget before the board, not about replacing everything. By month 12, leadership should be able to answer four questions: where are we exposed, which suppliers can move with us, what breaks when we switch the new methods on, and what will the full programme cost [15, 18]?

Institutions that have started offer two lessons. First, use what you already have. Business continuity plans list the services that must be restored first after an outage, and those are almost always where this work matters most [17]. Second, test something difficult early. A trial on a modern web service will succeed and teach little; a trial involving an ageing core system, a fleet of ATMs or the hardware that holds payment keys will show the real timetable. Keep the first live pilot contained, for example to traffic between internal systems or a single link with a willing partner.

After the first year, the work proceeds in waves. Most systems will be updated through routine vendor upgrades, much as software is patched today. The difficult minority, meaning old systems, hardware that cannot be updated and data that must stay private for decades, will decide whether the programme succeeds, so it should shape the plan from the outset.

2.6 Quantum key distribution: a niche option, not a substitute

Quantum key distribution (QKD) works differently. It sends encryption keys between two locations on particles of light, so any attempt to intercept them leaves a detectable trace, and it needs no quantum computer.

Its limits are clear. QKD protects only the key exchange on one physical link, not the wider financial system; it still relies on secure equipment at each end, needs specialised infrastructure, and cannot stop stolen passwords, compromised devices or breaches elsewhere. Institutions may consider it for a small number of very high-security links. It is no alternative to post-quantum cryptography, which the UK National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) recommends as the main response; the NCSC does not support QKD for government or military use [21].

2.7 What leaders should do now

Quantum readiness touches budgets, procurement, data retention and customer trust, so the security team cannot deliver it alone. It stalls if nobody senior owns it.

Five questions any board can ask:

  1. Who owns this, and is it funded?
  2. Which of our services and suppliers rely on the vulnerable methods?
  3. Which of our data must stay private beyond 2035, and is it still travelling over unprotected links?
  4. Which critical suppliers have given us dated commitments, and which have not?
  5. How quickly could we change our cryptography if a weakness were announced tomorrow?

If the answer to any of these is that we don’t know, that is the most useful finding of all, and the reason to start now. As with the millennium bug, success will look as though nothing happened [17].

3. Towards quantum advantage: four areas worth testing

Financial institutions already use powerful computers to price products, measure risk and allocate capital. Problems arise when more precision, more scenarios or more constraints make a calculation too slow or too costly. Teams then simplify the model, narrow its scope or accept a slower answer, and some work is dropped because the computing is not available.

Quantum advantage means a quantum method doing a useful job better, faster or more cheaply than the best classical alternative, once the full cost of data preparation, computing time, validation and integration is counted. That last condition matters. Many reported gains compare a quantum method with a simple classical one; fewer survive comparison with the strongest classical tools. The evidence so far is mixed: some trials on real data have matched or beaten the classical methods used, others show classical methods well ahead, and the clearest theoretical gains need much larger, error-corrected machines [20, 19].

A useful snapshot came from IEEE Quantum Week in September 2026, where JPMorganChase, IBM, American Express, Intesa Sanpaolo, the Bank of Canada and others presented finance results. None of the runs on quantum hardware showed a clear win over classical methods. The only reported production saving came from a quantum-inspired method running on ordinary computers [47]. For institutions, that points to a practical route: start with quantum-inspired tools that can pay back now, and frame problems so they can move to quantum hardware when it is ready.

The four tracks of the Q-FINEX Quantum Finance Challenge shape what follows: optimisation, quantum machine learning, simulation and digital assets. For each one, the business problem comes first, then what quantum methods might offer, then what published work shows so far.

3.1 Optimisation: portfolios and capital

The business problem. Many financial decisions mean choosing the best combination from a very large number of options under many rules at once. A portfolio manager rebalancing a bond fund must respect liquidity, transaction costs, sector limits and client mandates. A treasury team allocating collateral must balance eligibility, concentration limits and funding costs. A bank allocating capital across business lines faces similar trade-offs. Classical optimisation software handles much of this well, but the hardest versions, with many yes-or-no choices that interact, can force teams to simplify.

What quantum might offer. Quantum and hybrid methods search large sets of combinations in a different way, and quantum-inspired methods, which borrow ideas from quantum physics but run on ordinary computers, can sometimes deliver gains today.

What the evidence shows. In September 2025, Vanguard and IBM reported that hybrid quantum and classical methods, run on an IBM quantum processor, built constrained bond portfolios at a larger scale than expected and could match or outperform the traditional methods they were compared with [26]. Earlier work using real market data found that both quantum processors and quantum-inspired methods could handle dynamic portfolio problems, with the quantum-inspired approach often the more practical [23]. Independent benchmarks also show that some popular quantum optimisation methods lose accuracy quickly as problems grow on today’s hardware [25].

The most concrete saving so far did not need a quantum computer. Intesa Sanpaolo reported that a quantum-inspired scheduler for refilling about 900 ATMs cut its cost of cash by 12.7 per cent over a year, with service levels unchanged [47]. On real quantum hardware, JPMorganChase ran a small portfolio problem on 12 error-protected qubits; it worked, but did not yet beat an unprotected run. The Bank of Canada reported a quantum optimiser beating a well-tuned classical method on a month of settlement data in a sandbox, a long way from live use [47].

So what? Quantum-inspired and hybrid methods are worth testing now on well-defined allocation problems. Any gain is likely to be specific to the problem and has to be shown against a strong classical solver.

3.2 Quantum machine learning: fraud and trading

The business problem. Fraud and money laundering hide among millions of ordinary transactions. Detection systems must flag the suspicious few without burying investigators in false alarms that waste their time and inconvenience customers. In trading, desks want to predict, for example, which quotes a client is likely to accept, so that they price better and win more business.

What quantum might offer. Quantum machine learning uses quantum circuits to represent data in ways that may expose patterns classical models miss. Whether that helps depends on how the data is loaded into the quantum computer, how the model is trained and whether it beats strong classical models.

What the evidence shows. The HSBC and IBM bond trial described in Section 1 used more than a million real requests for quotes; its gain appeared only on noisy hardware, and the cause is not yet fully understood [24, 46]. American Express and IBM tested quantum-derived features for predicting credit defaults on IBM hardware and found the results within the margin of error of the classical model [47]. For fraud, a study by IBM, CERN and IRIS Analytics on card transactions found a quantum model performed about as well as a leading classical model, with the best results from combining the two; the work was run on a simulator, and accuracy fell sharply when hardware noise was added [27]. Some earlier claims of quantum speed-ups in machine learning were later matched by new classical methods, a reminder to benchmark carefully [33].

So what? This is the most active area of experimentation with real financial data. Results vary widely, and independent benchmarking against the institution’s own production models is essential before drawing conclusions.

3.3 Simulation: pricing and risk

The business problem. A structured product’s value depends on how several asset prices might move over its life. To price it, a bank simulates thousands or millions of possible market paths and averages the payoffs, a technique called Monte Carlo simulation. The same approach drives value-at-risk, counterparty exposure and capital calculations. More precision and more realistic assumptions mean more computing, so risk teams often settle for less scenario coverage or less frequent updates than they would like.

What quantum might offer. Under standard assumptions, halving the error of a Monte Carlo estimate needs about four times as many samples. A quantum technique called amplitude estimation needs only about twice as many steps for the same improvement. In practice this could mean a more precise price, more scenarios, or faster recalculation for selected complex products.

What the evidence shows. This is the area with the strongest theoretical case. Researchers at IBM showed how quantum computers could estimate value-at-risk [29] and price options, with small demonstrations on real quantum hardware [28]. A detailed study by Goldman Sachs and IBM researchers estimated that a practical advantage in derivative pricing would need about 8,000 logical qubits running far faster than any machine today [30].

A related, nearer-term idea is quantum randomness. Monte Carlo models depend on long streams of random numbers, and HSBC lists quantum random number generation for Monte Carlo among its quantum proofs of concept [52]. In March 2025, JPMorganChase, Quantinuum and US national laboratories reported in Nature the first random numbers produced by a quantum computer and certified as genuinely random, with 71,313 bits verified using supercomputers [48]. Whether quantum randomness makes financial models more accurate, rather than simply more trustworthy, still needs careful testing against the best classical generators.

So what? The pay-off is real but further away than in the other tracks. Institutions can prepare now by identifying the books where faster or more precise valuation would be worth most, building in-house skills, and testing methods on simulators so they are ready when hardware improves.

3.4 Digital assets: stablecoins and tokens

The business problem. Banks, payment firms and market infrastructure are issuing tokenised deposits, stablecoins and tokenised securities, and settling them on shared ledgers. These assets are secured almost entirely by digital signatures. Once issued, they can be hard to upgrade, because every holder, wallet and network participant must change together.

What quantum means here. This track has two sides. On the security side, tokens and ledgers built today need to be quantum-safe or easy to upgrade. On the opportunity side, the optimisation and simulation methods above may help manage liquidity, collateral and settlement across tokenised markets.

What the evidence shows. In 2024 HSBC piloted quantum-safe protection for its tokenised gold, including moving tokens between different ledgers [31]. In December 2025, the BIS Innovation Hub, the Bank of Italy, Banque de France, Deutsche Bundesbank, Swift and Nexi-Colt tested post-quantum signatures in an operational payment system and found significant performance differences that need further preparation [32]. On the threat side, a 2026 Google Quantum AI study estimated that the elliptic-curve cryptography behind Bitcoin and Ethereum could be broken with fewer than 500,000 physical qubits, and about 1.7 million bitcoin sit in older addresses whose public keys are already exposed, or as many as 6.7 million if reused addresses are counted [22, 54]. Ethereum developers are targeting protocol-level quantum protection by 2029 [39], and the HKMA has linked quantum preparedness directly to Hong Kong’s tokenisation plans [14].

So what? Anyone issuing or holding tokenised assets should build in the ability to change signature methods now, while designs are still young. The advantage questions in this track are mostly about optimising liquidity and settlement, and remain early research.

4. Quantum regulation: what financial institutions need to do

The immediate priority is preparing for post-quantum cryptography. Authorities are issuing standards and migration guidance, but national targets are not automatically legal deadlines for every financial institution. Institutions should check which rules apply in each market where they operate.

Security: guidance and timelines

Advantage: faster calculations still need validation

Quantum methods may improve pricing, optimisation and risk calculations, but they must meet the same controls as any other model: validation, documented limits, ongoing monitoring and, where needed, supervisory approval. A successful experiment does not make a model fit for production or for regulatory capital calculations [34]. The Basel framework creates demand for reliable risk measurement; it does not endorse any particular computing method [35]. Faster computation is a research opportunity, not a compliance shortcut.

Responsibility: the institution remains accountable

Accountability does not pass to a quantum vendor or cloud provider. APRA places final responsibility for information security on the board [36], and DORA keeps responsibility with financial entities when they outsource technology services [37]. Liability for any failure depends on the law, the contracts and the circumstances.

Executives should:

  • appoint an accountable owner and fund a prioritised migration plan;
  • assess supplier readiness and security controls rather than rely on assurances; and
  • require proper validation before quantum-generated results influence material financial decisions.

Quantum regulation and policy will be discussed at the Singapore FinTech Festival 2026, in dedicated sessions at Black Swan Summit in Perth in 2027, and in the second edition of this report.

No institution can do this alone, which is why Q-FINEX brings researchers, quantum companies, financial institutions and policy makers together as an ecosystem of ecosystems.

5. Q-FINEX: a trusted path for institutions, a door into finance for the quantum industry, and an ecosystem of ecosystems

Most institutions face the same obstacles when they try to act on quantum: limited in-house skills, uncertainty about which problems are worth testing, difficulty getting access to hardware and specialists, and no easy way to judge vendor claims. Running a credible experiment means bringing together a real business problem, safely governed data, a strong classical benchmark, quantum expertise and an independent evaluation. Few institutions can assemble all of that alone. Skills are a real constraint: in ISACA’s 2025 poll, only 7 per cent of professionals said they had a strong understanding of the new NIST post-quantum standards, and 44 per cent had never heard of them [44].

Q-FINEX, which began as the quantum finance experimentation testbed developed by GFTN with the University of Western Australia and ST Engineering, provides that structure. It takes a defined problem from readiness assessment to testing and, where the evidence justifies it, to a path towards implementation. It brings together financial institutions, market operators, researchers, technology providers and policy makers.

5.1 For financial institutions: how to start

For institutions that do not yet know where or how to begin, the first step is the Q-FINEX self-assessment app. Based on the five quick-start questions in Section 1, it gives a simple profile of where the institution stands on both resilience and advantage, and points to a next step. That step might be:

  • an executive briefing for the board or leadership team;
  • a readiness review covering cryptography, suppliers and skills;
  • help defining a security or business problem precise enough to test; or
  • a place in a Q-FINEX project or as a Quantum Finance Challenge sponsor.

5.2 Building capability together

Quantum readiness is as much about people as technology, and no single organisation holds all the skills it needs. Q-FINEX works because each community brings something the others lack and gains something it could not build alone. Figure 11 sets out the exchange for financial institutions, quantum and security companies, researchers, students and start-ups, and regulators and policy makers.

Behind these exchanges sits a wider network: research expertise at the University of Western Australia, engineering and security expertise from ST Engineering, access to quantum hardware and software providers, the global talent pool attracted by the Quantum Finance Challenge, and the convening power of GFTN’s forums, including the Singapore FinTech Festival and Black Swan Summit.

5.3 An ecosystem of ecosystems

Q-FINEX began in 2025 as an idea for a testbed: a place to research, test and validate quantum methods on real financial problems. It grew quickly, driven by the problems institutions brought to it and by the people who came to help solve them. Along the way it uncovered, and began to shape, a wider set of communities that each pursue their own goals in quantum computing, sensing and communication: research institutions; quantum companies working on hardware, software, infrastructure and implementation; financial institutions; and regulators and policy makers. Q-FINEX engages with these communities, communicates across them and connects them, so that they can build capacity and capability together and solve problems in the two areas that matter most to finance: quantum safety and quantum advantage.

5.3.1 Example: how Q-FINEX creates value for quantum and security companies

Financial services offer quantum companies two markets on different clocks.

The first is security, and it is buying now. Every bank, insurer, payment firm and market operator has to find and replace its vulnerable cryptography over the next decade, with the most sensitive systems due by about 2030 (Section 2.2). That means demand for discovery tools, post-quantum products, upgraded key-management hardware, migration services and testing. The supplier questions in Section 2.4 describe what these buyers will ask for: dated roadmaps, test results and contract commitments.

The second is computing, and it is still being proven. The value at stake in financial services is large (Section 1) [43], but Section 3 shows that institutions now expect evidence against strong classical methods, not demonstrations. The companies that win their trust will be those that test on real problems, publish honest comparisons, and help institutions build skills along the way.

The obstacle for most quantum companies is reaching the right people with the right problem. Finance executives are busy and wary of hype, real data is closely guarded, and buying decisions involve risk, security and procurement teams as well as technologists. Q-FINEX addresses this directly. The Quantum Finance Challenge puts a company’s platform to work on problems set by financial institutions, with results judged against classical baselines and shown at the Singapore FinTech Festival and Black Swan Summit. Technology partners meet the executives who set budgets and see their tools used by a new generation of developers.

Part IV: Summary and conclusions

The quantum transition has two agendas, and both have started. The security threat is not waiting for the quantum computer: data copied today can be read later, signatures can be forged once current cryptography falls, and authorities from the G7 to Singapore expect high-risk systems to be quantum-safe by around 2030. The opportunity is real but still being proven: quantum methods are being tested on real bank data, the first production gains have come from quantum-inspired methods, and the strongest long-term case lies in pricing and risk. No institution can do this alone, which is why Q-FINEX brings researchers, quantum companies, financial institutions and policy makers together as an ecosystem of ecosystems.

What institutions should do in the next 12 to 24 months

Secure today. Name an accountable owner, map where vulnerable cryptography is used, rank data by how long it must stay private, and ask critical suppliers for dated commitments. With NIST deprecating today’s methods after 2030 and disallowing them after 2035, and most supervisors expecting high-risk systems to move by 2030, aim to finish the first year with a tested pilot and a funded multi-year plan. Issuers and holders of tokenised assets should make their signature methods replaceable from the start.

Prove what works. Choose one or two business problems where slow or costly calculation limits decisions. Set a strong classical benchmark first, then test quantum-inspired methods, which can pay back today, and hybrid quantum methods against it. Judge results on full costs, not headline speed.

Build your advantage. Develop in-house understanding across technology, risk and business teams, starting where the research is deepest: pricing and risk. Institutions that build skills and data foundations now will be able to act quickly when error-corrected machines arrive and the evidence for a use case becomes clear.

Shape policy. Engage with supervisors and industry bodies on timelines, shared testing of payment and settlement systems, and common supplier standards. A coordinated transition is cheaper and safer than a fragmented one.

Join the ecosystem. Researchers, peers, suppliers and regulators are all working on the same problems. Institutions that take part in shared testing and learn from each other will move faster, spend less and avoid repeating others’ mistakes.

What quantum and security companies should do

Learn how financial institutions buy: through risk, security and procurement teams as well as technologists, and on the basis of evidence. Prepare dated product roadmaps and test results for quantum-safe work. For computing, choose a real finance problem, compare honestly with the best classical method, and be ready to show the results to executives.

What researchers, students and start-ups should do

Work on real finance problems with real data, publish honest comparisons against strong classical methods, and use the Quantum Finance Challenge as a route to industry partners, pilots and jobs.

What regulators and policy makers should do

Keep timelines clear and aligned across jurisdictions, support shared testing of critical systems such as payments and settlement, and use evidence from industry pilots to set realistic expectations.

  1. Take the Q-FINEX self-assessment to see where your institution stands and what to do next.
  2. Join the Quantum Finance Challenge as a track sponsor, technology partner or prize partner.
  3. Come to Black Swan Summit Perth, 5 to 7 April 2027, the home of Q-FINEX, to see the results and meet the whole ecosystem in one place. Register your interest at australia.blackswansummit.com.
